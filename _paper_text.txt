I. INTRODUCTION
Motile cilia are slender, actively beating organelles that generate fluid motion in biological processes ranging from
mucociliary clearance and cerebrospinal-fluid transport to feeding, pumping and locomotion in microorganisms [1–4].
Atthescaleofindividualciliarybeats,thesurroundingfluidistypicallygovernedbyStokesflow,wheretime-reversible
reciprocal actuation cannot, by itself, produce net locomotion, as captured by the scallop theorem [5, 6]. A single
motile cilium generates pumping by executing a non-reciprocal beat, usually consisting of an effective stroke away
from the surface and a recovery stroke closer to the surface [7, 8]. In ciliary arrays, an additional level of organisation
arises when neighbouring cilia beat with a phase difference, forming metachronal waves [9–11]. Waves propagating in
the direction of the effective stroke are termed symplectic, whereas those propagating in the opposite direction are
termed antiplectic [12]. The pumping performance of such arrays is thus characterised by a cycle-averaged flow rate
that depends on both the individual beat and the phase relation between neighbouring cilia.
Previous studies have provided important insights into metachronal coordination in autonomous or self-sustained
ciliary systems. Early reduced models represented ciliary dynamics using hydrodynamically coupled oscillators or
rotors. These studies identified several ingredients that allow fluid-mediated interactions to generate synchronisation
andphaselocking,includingbeatgeometry[13],oscillatorcompliance[14],andgenericphase-responseconditions[15].
Extensionstochainsandcarpetsfurtherdemonstratedtravellingmetachronalwavesandcollectivephasedynamicsin
largerarrays[16,17]. Buildingonthesereduceddescriptions,recenttheorieshaveconnectedlocalciliarymechanicsto
large-scalephasestructures: beatandarraygeometrycanselectstablewavevectors[18],multiscalebiophysicalmodels
describe discrete metachronal-wave states [19], and large-scale carpet models link spontaneous phase coordination to
fluid pumping [20]. Wave characteristics can also be shaped by elasticity and surface effects [21], while near-field
hydrodynamic interactions can select the travelling-wave direction [22]. Together, these studies have substantially
clarified how metachronal waves emerge, persist and acquire their collective phase structure in self-organised ciliary
systems.
When the beat kinematics and phase relation are prescribed or externally controlled, the cycle-averaged flow rate
can be examined directly as a function of phase coordination. Computational studies in a range of ciliary flow
settings have repeatedly identified antiplectic coordination as advantageous for transport or swimming performance.
In ciliary carpets, antiplectic coordination enhances transport and mixing compared with symplectic coordination
[23]. The same trend has been reported in models of mucociliary transport in a two-phase flow [24–26], as well as
for ciliated swimmers [27, 28]. Artificial cilia provide a particularly clear setting in which to interpret this phase

gives an analytical criterion for flow-maximising phase selection by linking this negative shift to the balance between
wall-mediated horizontal and vertical flow responses, thereby explaining how spacing and beat geometry alter the
preferred phase relation, including a possible shift towards symplectic coordination in the tilted-slider model.
II. PROBLEM STATEMENT AND METHOD
Consider M cilia models arranged in a one-dimensional array along the x-axis with a constant inter-cilium spacing
ℓ, and figure 1 illustrates the problem setup for the cases (a) M =1 and (b) M =2. Each cilium has a resting length
L and is modelled as a bead–spring chain anchored at its base to a planar no-slip wall at z = 0. The surrounding
fluid is an incompressible Newtonian fluid with the viscosity µ and the density ρ, and the flow is assumed to be in
the Stokes regime since the Reynolds number is Re=L2fρ/µ≪1, where f is the typical beating frequency.
The cilia are driven by active torques τ applied at the joints, as illustrated in figure 1(b). The main objective of
thisworkistounderstandthecollectivedynamicsofciliathatmaximisethenetfluidpumpinginx-direction;inother
words, to elucidate the time-dependent active torque inputs τ(t) that maximise the pumping. Note that although we
solve the full three-dimensional dynamics of the system, all beads and springs remain in the same xz-plane due to
symmetry; the active torques are applied only about the y-axis. The reinforcement learning framework is introduced
to search for a near-optimal actuation pattern.

IV. MECHANISM OF FLOW ENHANCEMENT
In the previous section, reinforcement learning showed that antiplectic coordination is preferred over the explored
parameter range of cilia number, cilia spacing, and bending stiffness. Although reinforcement learning can search
for the flow-maximising coordination under each condition, it does not by itself reveal the underlying physics of
the obtained solution. This makes it difficult to assess the generality of the obtained coordination and, hence, to
understand how the optimal coordination varies across parameter space, including the transition between symplectic
and antiplectic coordination.
Tocomplementthislimitation,wenowintroduceareducedtilted-slidermodelinwhicheachciliumisrepresentedby
abeadconstrainedtomovealongatiltedstraightlinenearano-slipwall. Thismodelisinspiredbyrower-typeminimal
models for hydrodynamically coupled cilia, which have proved useful for describing synchronisation and metachronal
coordination [17, 49, 50]. In contrast to these rower-type models, which employ geometric switching between stroke
states,thepresentmodelisdrivenbyprescribedphase-laggedforcingtogetherwithanelasticrestoringresponse. The
tilt angle serves as a reduced descriptor of the mean beating orientation relevant to wall-bounded pumping, while the
detailed effective-recovery effective–recovery stroke asymmetry is ignored. In this section, we use this reduced model
to reveal analytically how the flow-maximising coordination depends on system parameters.

The above analysis shows that collective flow enhancement is associated with the negative shift induced by hydro-
dynamic coupling with neighbouring cilia. This negative shift provides a useful signature of enhanced transport, but
it does not by itself generate the enhancement. Rather, the flow enhancement arises through elastohydrodynamic
coupling, in which the negative shift modifies the elastic response of the cilia and thereby produces larger net flow.
V. CONCLUSIONS
We have investigated how hydrodynamic coupling and beat geometry determine the phase difference between
neighbouring cilia that maximises the cycle-averaged flow rate in wall-bounded Stokes flow. We addressed this
problem by first using reinforcement learning to optimise the cilia model without prescribing either the beat pattern
or the phase relation. Guided by the resulting optimised motions, we then developed a reduced tilted-slider model
that allows a weak-coupling perturbation analysis of phase-dependent flow enhancement.
In the cilia model, reinforcement learning optimisation of a single cilium produced a non-reciprocal beating motion
with distinct effective and recovery strokes. For larger arrays, the optimisation selected antiplectic metachronal
coordination as the flow-maximising state, with approximately uniform nearest-neighbour phase differences. For
two cilia, the phase-sweep analysis reproduced most of the enhancement obtained by direct reinforcement learning
optimisation. These results show that the imposed phase difference accounts for most of the flow enhancement, while
allowing the actuation of the two cilia to be optimised independently provides only a smaller additional contribution.
The tilted-slider model isolates the mechanism behind this phase dependence. In this model, each cilium is repre-
sentedbyabeadconstrainedtomovereciprocallyalongatiltedlinenearano-slipwall,withthetiltparametrisingthe
excursiongeometryrelativetothewall. Aweak-couplingperturbationanalysisyieldsananalyticalexpressionforthe
cycle-averaged flow rate. The analysis shows that an imposed phase difference produces a shift in the time-averaged
ciliary position opposite to the effective-stroke direction. This negative shift enters the leading elastic contribution to
the flow-rate expression, and therefore provides the main contribution to the phase-dependent change in the cycle-
averaged flow rate. The same analysis shows that the flow-maximising phase difference is determined by the balance
between the wall-mediated horizontal and vertical flow responses. This explains why small tilts favour antiplectic
coordination whereas larger tilts can move the optimum towards the symplectic side in the tilted-slider model.
These findings complement previous interpretations based on near-field shielding, direct obstruction and instanta-
neousspacingbetweenneighbouringciliabyprovidingacycle-averagedinterpretationofthesamephysicalasymmetry.
In particular, the empirical tip-spacing metric proposed by Dong et al. [30] predicts larger flow when neighbouring
tips are farther apart during the power stroke and closer during the recovery stroke. The present analysis identifies
the hydrodynamic mechanism behind this rule: close approach during the recovery stroke strengthens the interaction
that drives the negative shift, whereas the forward shift during the effective stroke is weaker because the cilia are
farther apart. The antiplectic optimum is therefore selected not by the wave direction alone, but by whether the
phase relation produces this negative shift.
The weak-coupling theory makes this interpretation predictive. It gives an analytical criterion for flow-maximising
phase selection by linking the negative shift to the balance between wall-mediated horizontal and vertical flow re-
sponses. This criterion explains both how spacing shifts the optimal phase difference and how beat geometry can
weaken or reverse the antiplectic preference. It also suggests a design principle for artificial ciliary pumps: the phase
difference,spacingandbeatgeometryshouldbechosentogeneratethenegativeshiftthatenhancesthecycle-averaged
flow rate. In biological or self-sustained ciliary systems, the same shift is accessible from kinematic imaging and can
therefore provide a link between metachronal-wave coordination and pumping function when direct flow-rate mea-
surements are difficult. Thus, the present study identifies the negative shift as a mechanistic and observable quantity
linking metachronal phasing to flow generation in wall-bounded Stokes flow.
